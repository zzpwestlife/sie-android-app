## 1.1 经济周期与常见经济指标（**&#x20;Business Cycle & Common Economic indicator Learning Material**）

### 1.1.1 经济周期基础知识（**Fundamentals of the Business Cycle&#x20;**）

![](<images/入门考试学习资料 Learning Resources for Entry Knowledge Test-image1.png>)

**The business cycle refers to the recurring, irregular fluctuations in aggregate economic activity around the economy’s long-term potential growth trend.&#x20;**&#x49;t is officially determined by the U.S. National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER), which defines a "recession" as a significant decline in economic activity spread across the economy, lasting more than a few months—encompassing production, employment, and income. The business cycle’s duration varies (typically 2–10 years) and is driven by shifts in aggregate demand and aggregate supply.

| **Stage**                   | **Core Economic Characteristics&#x20;**<br />                                                                                                                | **Key Indicator Trends&#x20;**<br />                                                                                                    | **U.S. Market Relevance&#x20;**<br />                                                 |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Expansion**               | Economic activity rises above potential output; real GDP grows steadily; employment expands; business investment increases; consumer confidence strengthens. | Real GDP growth: 2%–3% (U.S. long-term trend; Unemployment rate: Below natural rate (4%–4.5%); Core inflation: 1%–2% (near Fed target). | 2009–2020 U.S. expansion                                                              |
| **Peak**                    | Economic growth slows to a halt; output reaches maximum level; inflationary pressures peak as capacity constraints emerge.                                   | Real GDP growth: <1%; Unemployment rate: Trough (often <4%); Core inflation: >2.5% (above target).                                      | February 2020 U.S. peak                                                               |
| **Contraction (Recession)** | Economic activity falls below potential output; real GDP declines; businesses cut investment; layoffs increase; consumer spending weakens.                   | Real GDP growth: Negative for ≥2 quarters; Unemployment rate: Rises ≥1 percentage point; Core inflation: Decelerates.                   | 2007–2009 Global Financial Crisis recession, U.S. unemployment rose from 4.7% to 10%. |
| **Trough**                  | Economic decline stops; output hits bottom; pessimism fades; businesses and consumers begin to rebuild confidence.                                           | Real GDP growth: Stabilizes near 0%; Unemployment rate: Peaks; Core inflation: May reach a floor                                        | April 2020 U.S. trough                                                                |

### 1.1.3 经济周期的关键驱动因素（**Key Drivers of the U.S. Business Cycle**）

Factors directly influencing U.S. business cycle fluctuations

* **Consumer Spending**: Consumer spending accounts for approximately 68%–70% of U.S. GDP, making it the primary driver of aggregate demand. Changes in consumer confidence (tied to employment security and wage growth, per textbook) directly impact retail sales, housing demand, and discretionary spending (e.g., autos, electronics)—critical for U.S. equity sectors like consumer staples and discretionary.

* **Business Investment**: Includes spending on machinery, equipment, and commercial real estate. U.S. Business investment is sensitive to interest rates (Fed policy), corporate profit margins, and technological innovation (e.g., AI-related investment boom in 2023–2024).

* **Monetary Policy (Federal Reserve)**: Fed’s adjustments to the federal funds rate and balance sheet (quantitative easing/QE, quantitative tightening/QT) directly influence borrowing costs for businesses and consumers, asset prices (stocks, bonds), and inflation.

* **Net Exports**: Tied to the U.S. dollar exchange rate and global demand. A stronger USD (e.g., 2022) reduces U.S. export competitiveness, while weak global demand (e.g., 2023 Eurozone slowdown) weighs on U.S. export growth.

## 1.2 货币政策（**Monetary Policy/U.S. Fiscal Policy**）

This chapter is designed for firm employees to understand the Federal Reserve's (Fed) interest rate policies,**&#x20;specifically rate hikes and cuts.** It also covers the objectives and implementation of the U.S. fiscal policy.

### 1.2.1 **美联储货币政策概述（Federal Reserve Monetary Policy Overview）**

The Federal Reserve, through the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC), sets the target range for the federal funds rate—the interest rate at which banks lend reserves to each other overnight. This rate influences broader borrowing costs across the economy, including mortgages, corporate loans, and consumer credit. The **Fed's dual mandate is to promote maximum employment and stable prices (targeting 2% inflation).**

Historical fed funds rate cycles since 1980 include multiple hikes and cuts, often in response to economic conditions. For instance:

* **Hike Cycles**: Examples include 1994-1995 (from 3% to 6%), 1999-2000 (4.75% to 6.5%), 2004-2006 (1% to 5.25%), 2015-2018 (0.25% to 2.5%), and 2022-2023 (0.25% to 5.5%).

* **Cut Cycles**: Notable periods include 2001 (6.5% to 1.75%), 2007-2008 (5.25% to 0-0.25%), 2019 (2.5% to 1.75%), and 2024-2025 (5.5% to approximately 4-4.25% by late 2025).

These cycles reflect the Fed's reactive approach: tightening during expansions to curb inflation and easing during slowdowns to stimulate growth.

### 1.2.2 **主要货币工具（Key Monetary Policy Tools）**

#### **Open Market Operations (OMO)**&#x20;

OMO is one of the Fed's oldest and most traditional tools. It involves the buying and selling of government securities (primarily U.S. Treasury bonds and mortgage-backed securities) in the open market to adjust the level of reserves in the banking system.

* **How it works**: When the Fed buys securities (expansionary OMO), it injects money into the economy by crediting banks' reserve accounts, increasing the money supply and lowering interest rates. Conversely, selling securities (contractionary OMO) withdraws reserves, reducing the money supply and raising rates.

* **Role in policy**: OMO helps the Fed achieve its target federal funds rate. During quantitative easing (QE) programs, like those in 2008–2014 and 2020–2022, the Fed used large-scale asset purchases to lower long-term interest rates and support economic recovery. In normal times, OMO fine-tunes short-term rates.

* **Example**: In response to the COVID-19 pandemic, the Fed expanded its balance sheet through OMO to provide liquidity and stabilize markets.

#### **Interest On Reserve Balances (IORB)**&#x20;

Introduced in 2008, IORB is the interest rate the Fed pays on reserves that banks hold at Federal Reserve Banks. This includes both required reserves (a small fraction of deposits) and excess reserves.

* **How it works**: By setting the IORB rate, the Fed establishes a "floor" for short-term interest rates. Banks are unlikely to lend reserves below this rate since they can earn IORB risk-free. Raising IORB tightens policy by encouraging banks to hold reserves rather than lend, while lowering it encourages lending.

* **Role in policy**: In the ample-reserves framework (post-2008), IORB is the primary tool for controlling the federal funds rate. It replaced reliance on scarce reserves and direct OMO for day-to-day adjustments. As of 2026, the IORB rate is adjusted during FOMC meetings to align with economic conditions.

* **Example**: If inflation rises, the Fed might hike IORB to 5% from 4.5%, pulling up market rates and slowing economic activity.

#### **Overnight Reverse Repurchase Agreements (ONRRP)**&#x20;

The ONRRP facility allows eligible counterparties (like money market funds and government-sponsored enterprises) to lend cash to the Fed overnight in exchange for Treasury securities as collateral. It's essentially a way for the Fed to borrow short-term funds.

* **How it works**: The Fed sets an offering rate for ONRRP, which acts as a supplementary floor for short-term rates. Participants deposit funds at this rate, reducing excess liquidity in the market. Increasing the ONRRP rate absorbs more liquidity, while decreasing it releases it.

* **Role in policy**: ONRRP complements IORB by providing a floor for non-bank institutions that can't earn IORB directly. It's crucial in managing abundant reserves and preventing rates from falling too low. Usage surged in 2021–2023 as liquidity flooded the system from QE.

* **Example**: During periods of high liquidity (e.g., post-stimulus), the Fed raises the ONRRP rate to 0.05% above the target floor, ensuring money market rates stay aligned with policy goals.

#### **Discount Rate**

The discount rate is the interest rate charged to commercial banks and other depository institutions for short-term loans from the Fed's discount window. There are three tiers: primary credit (for healthy banks), secondary credit (for troubled ones), and seasonal credit.

* **How it works**: Banks borrow from the discount window as a last resort for liquidity needs. The primary discount rate is typically set 0.5–1% above the federal funds target, acting as a ceiling for rates. Lowering it signals easier credit, while raising it tightens conditions.

* **Role in policy**: It's a backup tool for emergency liquidity, reducing stigma through reforms post-2008. It supports financial stability rather than day-to-day rate control but influences overall borrowing costs.

* **Example**: In crises like 2008 or 2020, the Fed cut the discount rate to near zero to encourage borrowing and prevent bank runs.

### 1.2.3 **How These Tools Interact in Policy Implementation**

The Fed uses these tools in concert within its ample-reserves regime:

* **Setting the target range**: The FOMC announces a target range for the federal funds rate (e.g., 5.25–5.50%).

* **Floor system**: IORB and ONRRP keep rates from falling below the target by providing attractive returns on reserves and repo transactions.

* **Ceiling and adjustments**: The discount rate caps rates from rising too high, while OMO adds or removes reserves as needed for fine-tuning.

* **Quantitative tools**: In extraordinary times, large-scale OMO (QE or quantitative tightening) expands or shrinks the balance sheet to influence longer-term rates and asset prices.

| **Tool**          | **Primary Function**        | **Expansionary Use**              | **Contractionary Use**                  | **Key Interaction**                                 |
| ----------------- | --------------------------- | --------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| **OMO**           | Adjust reserve levels       | Buy securities (inject liquidity) | Sell securities (withdraw liquidity)    | Complements IORB/ONRRP for balance sheet management |
| **IORB**          | Set floor for rates<br />   | Lower rate (encourage lending)    | Raise rate (encourage holding reserves) | Main tool for daily rate control                    |
| **ONRRP**         | Absorb excess liquidity     | Lower rate (release funds)<br />  | Raise rate (absorb funds)               | Supports IORB for non-banks                         |
| **Discount Rate** | Provide emergency liquidity | Lower rate (cheaper borrowing)    | Raise rate (discourage borrowing)       | Acts as a safety net ceiling                        |

## 1.3 **美国财政政策（Introduction to U.S. Fiscal Policy）**

### 1.3.1&#x20;**&#x20;基础概念（Policy Logic and Key Concepts）**

**Fiscal policy&#x20;**&#x72;efers to the decisions made by the U.S. government regarding taxation and spending to influence the overall economy. It is managed primarily by Congress and the President, with the goal of achieving macroeconomic stability, sustainable growth, full employment, and price stability (i.e., controlling inflation). Unlike monetary policy, which is handled by the Federal Reserve through interest rates and money supply, fiscal policy uses the federal budget as its main tool. The underlying logic is that government actions can smooth out economic fluctuations, support long-term prosperity, and address inequalities.

### 1.3.2 **核心目标（Core Objectives of U.S. Fiscal Policy）**

The policy logic is rooted in Keynesian economics, which argues that governments should actively intervene to manage demand in the economy. **The main goals include:**

* **Economic Growth**: Stimulating activity during slowdowns.

* **Full Employment**: Reducing unemployment by creating jobs.

* **Price Stability**: Preventing high inflation or deflation.

* **Income Redistribution**: Using taxes and spending to reduce inequality.

These objectives are pursued throug&#x68;**&#x20;two primary tools:**

* **Taxation**: Adjusting tax rates (e.g., income, corporate, or sales taxes) to influence how much money people and businesses have to spend.

* **Government Spending**: Allocating funds for programs like infrastructure, education, defense, or social welfare.

The U.S. Treasury Department implements these, often through annual budgets proposed by the President and approved by Congress.

### 1.3.3 **财政政策类型（Types of Fiscal Policy）: Expansionary vs. Contractionary**

Fiscal policy can be **expansionary** (to boost the economy) or **contractionary** (to cool it down). The choice depends on the economic cycle—boom times vs. recessions.

* **Expansionary Fiscal Policy**: This involves increasing government spending or cutting taxes to inject more money into the economy, raising aggregate demand (total spending by consumers, businesses, and government). It's used when the economy is weak, such as during high unemployment or low growth.

* **Contractionary Fiscal Policy**: This means decreasing spending or raising taxes to reduce aggregate demand, which helps control inflation during economic booms.

**Example**: Imagine the economy as a car engine. Expansionary policy is like pressing the gas pedal to speed up (e.g., more spending on roads creates jobs). Contractionary policy is like easing off the gas to avoid overheating (e.g., higher taxes reduce excessive consumer spending).

### 1.3.4 **逆周期调节（Countercyclical Regulation）: The Key Stabilizing Logic**

* A central principle in the U.S. Fiscal policy is **countercyclical regulation** (also called countercyclical fiscal policy). This means the government acts in the opposite direction of the economic cycle to stabilize it:

* During a **recession** (economic downturn), the government adopts expansionary measures to counteract falling demand.

* During an **expansion** (economic boom), it shifts to contractionary measures to prevent overheating, like bubbles in asset prices or runaway inflation.

This logic helps avoid extreme swings, promoting steady growth. Automatic stabilizers (built-in features like unemployment benefits that rise during recessions) enhance this, but discretionary actions (deliberate policy changes) are often needed.

> **Specific Case Study: The 2008-2009 Global Financial Crisis**
>
> **Context**: The U.S. The economy entered a severe recession due to the housing market collapse and banking failures. Unemployment soared to 10%, and GDP (gross domestic product) shrank by about 4%.
>
> **Countercyclical Response**: Under President Obama, Congress passed the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act (ARRA) in 2009, an expansionary package worth $831 billion. This included:
>
> Tax cuts for individuals and businesses to increase disposable income (e.g., payroll tax reductions, putting more money in workers' pockets).
>
> Increased spending on infrastructure (e.g., roads, bridges, and schools), creating jobs in construction.
>
> Extended unemployment benefits, acting as an automatic stabilizer to support consumer spending.
>
> **Outcome**: This helped shorten the recession, adding millions of jobs and boosting GDP growth. Without it, economists estimate unemployment could have hit 12-15%. It exemplified countercyclical logic: When private sector demand collapsed, government stepped in to fill the gap.

> **Another Example: The COVID-19 Pandemic Response (2020-2021)**
>
> **Context**: The pandemic caused a sharp economic shutdown, with unemployment jumping to 14.8% in April 2020 and businesses closing en masse.
>
> **Countercyclical Measures**: Multiple stimulus bills were enacted, totaling over $5 trillion:
>
> The CARES Act (2020) under President Trump provided direct payments ($1,200 per adult) to boost consumer spending, expanded unemployment insurance (adding $600 weekly), and loans to small businesses via the Paycheck Protection Program (PPP).
>
> The American Rescue Plan (2021) under President Biden added more direct payments ($1,400), child tax credits, and funding for vaccines and schools.
>
> **Logic in Action**: These expansionary steps countered the cycle by maintaining demand when private activity halted. For instance, direct payments helped families pay bills, preventing deeper poverty and supporting retail sales. Result: The economy recovered faster than expected, with unemployment dropping to 3.9% by late 2021.

### 1.3.5 **Challenges and Considerations in Fiscal Policy Logic**

While effective, fiscal policy isn't perfect:

* **Timing and Political Delays**: Policies take time to implement; Congress debates can slow responses.

* **Debt Implications**: Expansionary policies often increase the national debt. The logic is that short-term borrowing is justified if it prevents long-term damage, but sustained deficits can lead to higher interest rates.

* **Coordination with Monetary Policy**: Fiscal actions work best alongside Fed policies, like low interest rates during recessions.

## 1.4 **货币、财政政策背后的逻辑，以及不同经济环境/政策机制下的资产绩效。(The Logic Behind Monetary and Fiscal Policy, and Asset performance Under Different Economic Environment/Policy Regime.)**

The focus is on the underlying logic of monetary/fiscal policy and their implications for key assets: U.S. equities (represented by the S\&P 500) and U.S. Treasuries (focusing on the 10-Year Treasury yield and bond prices). Rate changes do not have uniform effects; outcomes depend on economic contexts, such as soft landings versus recessions.&#x20;

### 1.4.1&#x20;**&#x20;美国应对经济周期波动的政策（U.S. Policy Responses to Business Cycle Fluctuations）**

#### **Monetary Policy (Federal Reserve)**

* **Expansion/Inflationary Pressures:&#x20;**&#x46;ed hikes the federal funds rate to increase borrowing costs, cool economic growth, and curb inflation (e.g., 2022–2023 rate hikes: 0.25% to 5.25% to combat post-COVID inflation). QT (reducing Fed’s bond holdings) is used to tighten liquidity, pressuring long-term bond yields and equity valuations.

* **Contraction/Recession:&#x20;**&#x46;ed cuts the federal funds rate to lower borrowing costs, stimulate spending and investment (e.g., 2020 rate cut to near 0% during COVID recession). QE (purchasing Treasury and mortgage-backed securities) injects liquidity, lowers long-term yields, and supports asset prices.

#### **Fiscal Policy (U.S. Government)**

* **Contraction/Recession**: Expansionary fiscal policy—tax cuts (e.g., 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act) and increased government spending (e.g., 2020 CARES Act, $2.2 trillion in stimulus) to boost aggregate demand. This increases U.S. Treasury issuance, potentially pressuring bond yields if supply outpaces demand.

* **Expansion/High Debt**: Restrictive fiscal policy (rarely used in practice) – tax hikes or spending cuts to reduce budget deficits. The U.S. often runs deficits even during expansions, impacting long-term Treasury supply.

### 1.4.2 **美国金融市场在曾整个经济周期中的表现（U.S. Financial Market Performance Across the Business Cycle）**

Critical for investment decisions—asset class returns vary systematically across cycle stages.

#### **膨胀阶段 Expansion Stage**

* **Stocks**: Outperform other assets. CFA Curriculum notes growth stocks (e.g., technology, consumer discretionary) tend to lead as corporate profits rise—S\&P 500 average annual return: 14%–16% during expansions.

* **Bonds**: Moderate returns. Short-term bonds outperform long-term bonds as the Fed may hike rates to curb inflation.

* **Commodities**: Strong returns. Industrial metals (e.g., copper) benefit from increased business investment; energy prices rise with consumer and industrial demand.

#### **高峰至收缩（衰退）阶段 Peak to Contraction (Recession) Stage**

* **Stocks**: Decline sharply. Defensive stocks (consumer staples, healthcare, utilities) outperform as investors seek stability. S\&P 500 average annual return: -18% to -22% during recessions.

* **Bonds**: Safe-haven assets. Long-term U.S. Treasuries outperform as the Fed cuts rates—10-year Treasury average yield falls by 1.4%–1.6 percentage points during recessions; prices rise accordingly.

* **Commodities**: Underperform. Demand for industrial commodities collapses; energy prices drop amid reduced consumption.

#### **从低谷期到早期扩张阶段 Trough to Early Expansion Stage**

* **Stocks**: Rebound strongly. Cyclical stocks (e.g., industrials, financials) lead as economic optimism returns—S\&P 500 average return in the first 12 months post-trough: 23%–27% (CFA textbook data).

* **Bonds**: Still positive but underperform stocks. Short-term bonds gain as rates stabilize; long-term bonds return moderate.

* **Commodities**: Rebound. Industrial metals lead as business investment resumes; energy prices rise with economic activity.

### 1.4.3 **货币政策对美国股市的影响 Impacts of monetary policy on U.S. Equities (S\&P 500)**

Equities are sensitive to rate changes due to effects on corporate earnings, discount rates, and investor sentiment.

* **During Rate Hikes:** Stocks often face headwinds as higher borrowing costs compress profit margins and slow growth. The S\&P 500 averaged monthly returns of 0.88% from 1991-2015, but performance varied; in rising rate periods, returns were higher (1.26%) than in declining periods (0.73%), though volatility increased. In 2022, amid hikes, the S\&P 500 fell nearly 20%. However, early-stage hikes can see continued rallies if fundamentals remain strong. (Source: Mackenzie Investments)

* **During Rate Cuts**: Equities generally perform well in non-recessionary environments, with average 1-year returns of almost 20% post-first cut in soft landings. In recessionary cuts, returns can turn negative. Since 1974, the S\&P 500 rallied in most 1-year periods post-first cut, but fell during recessions (e.g., 2001, 2008). In 2024-2025 cuts, the S\&P 500 gained amid constructive growth. (Source: Mackenzie Investments)

### 1.4.4 **货币政策对美国国债的影响&#x20;**&#x49;mpacts of monetary on U.S. Treasuries (10-Year Yield and Bonds)

Bonds move inversely to yields; rate changes affect prices and the yield curve.

* **During Rate Hikes:** Bond prices fall as yields rise to compete with new issues. The 10-Year Treasury yield increased by an average of 53 basis points during the last four hike cycles. In 2022, yields surged from around 1.5% to over 4%, causing bond losses.

* **During Rate Cuts**: Bonds rally as yields fall, especially longer-duration ones. The yield curve often steepens, with short-end yields dropping more&#x20;

### 2.4.1 **股息 (Dividends)**

* **What Are Dividends?**

Dividends are payments that a company makes to its shareholders from its profits. They are like a share of the company's earnings distributed to owners of the stock. Not all companies pay dividends—many growth-focused companies reinvest profits instead—but mature, profitable companies often do. Dividends are usually paid quarterly (every three months), but can also be semi-annual or annual.

* **Dividend Yield**: This is the annual dividend amount divided by the stock price, shown as a percentage. It tells investors how much return they're getting from dividends alone.

* **Why Companies Pay Dividends**: To reward shareholders and signal financial health. It can attract income-focused investors, like retirees.

* **Impact on Investors**: Dividends provide regular income. They're taxed as ordinary income or at a lower rate if qualified (held for over 60 days). Reinvesting dividends can compound growth over time.

### 2.4.2 **股票分割 (Stock Splits)**

* **What Are Stock Splits?**

A stock split is when a company increases the number of its shares by dividing existing ones. For example, in a 2-for-1 split, each share becomes two, and the price halves. The total value of your investment stays the same right after the split—it's like cutting a pizza into more slices without adding more pizza.

* **Key Things to Know:**

* **Common Ratios**: 2-for-1, 3-for-1, or 4-for-1 are typical. Reverse splits (e.g., 1-for-10) combine shares to raise the price, often for low-priced stocks.

* **Why Companies Do Splits**: To make shares more affordable and attract more investors. High stock prices (e.g., over $500) can seem expensive, so splitting lowers the per-share price without changing the company's value.

* **Impact on Investors**: No immediate gain or loss in value, but it can increase liquidity (easier trading) and sometimes boost stock price due to positive perception. Dividends and earnings per share adjust accordingly.

* **Announcement and Effective Date**: Companies announce splits in advance; the split happens on the effective date, and prices adjust automatically.

### 2.4.3 **股票回购 Stock Buybacks**

* **What Are Stock Buybacks?**

Stock buybacks (or share repurchases) occur when a company uses its cash to buy back its own shares from the market. This reduces the number of outstanding shares, effectively giving remaining shareholders a larger ownership stake.

* **Key Things to Know:**

* **How It Works**: Companies announce a buyback program with a set amount (e.g., $10 billion) and timeframe. They buy shares on the open market or through private deals.

* **Why Companies Do Buybacks**: To return cash to shareholders (like dividends but more flexible), boost earnings per share (EPS) by spreading profits over fewer shares, and signal that management thinks the stock is undervalued.

* **Impact on Investors**: Can increase stock price over time due to higher EPS and perceived confidence. It's tax-efficient (no immediate tax like dividends) but doesn't provide cash unless you sell shares. However, it can be controversial if done instead of investing in growth.

* **Authorization**: Boards approve programs, but companies aren't required to complete them fully.

This reduced outstanding shares from about 25 billion in 2015 to around 14.84 billion by 2025, boosting EPS by about 2.62% annually from buybacks alone. For investors, this meant each remaining share represented a bigger slice of Apple's profits, helping drive long-term stock appreciation.

### auto. **并购与收购 Mergers & Acquisitions (M\&A)**

* **What Are Mergers & Acquisitions?**

Mergers happen when two companies combine into one (often as "equals," though one usually dominates). Acquisitions are when one company buys another, and the target becomes part of the buyer. M\&A deals can be friendly (agreed by both) or hostile (unwanted takeover).

* **Key Things to Know:**

* **Types**: Horizontal (same industry, e.g., competitors merging), vertical (supply chain, e.g., buyer acquires supplier), or conglomerate (unrelated businesses).

* **How It Works**: Deals involve cash, stock swaps, or both. They require board approval, shareholder votes, and regulatory review (e.g., antitrust checks by the FTC or DOJ).

* **Why Companies Do M\&A**: To grow faster, enter new markets, gain technology/assets, cut costs through synergies, or eliminate competition.

* **Impact on Investors**: Can boost stock prices if successful (e.g., more market power), but risks include integration failures, debt from financing, or culture clashes leading to value destruction. Target shareholders often get a premium (higher price) for their shares.

* **Announcement Effects**: Stocks of the target usually rise; the buyer's may fall short-term due to costs.

* **Example:** Microsoft Corporation Acquires Activision Blizzard (2023)

* In 2023, Microsoft Corporation (MSFT) acquired Activision Blizzard Inc. (ATVI), a leading video game publisher known for franchises like Call of Duty, Warcraft, and Candy Crush, in an all-cash deal valued at $68.7 billion (ultimately closing at $75.4 billion). The deal, announced on January 18, 2022, offered $95 per share, a 45% premium over ATVI's closing price of about $65 the day before. This strategic acquisition aimed to expand Microsoft's gaming ecosystem, including Xbox and Game Pass, by adding popular titles and strengthening its position against competitors like Sony and Tencent. The deal faced extensive regulatory scrutiny from bodies like the FTC, CMA (UK), and EU over antitrust concerns but was approved after concessions, closing on October 13, 2023.

**Stock Price Performance During the Process:**

* **Pre-Announcement (January 17, 2022)**: ATVI closed at around $65 per share. MSFT was trading at about $310.

* **Announcement Day (January 18, 2022)**: ATVI's stock surged nearly 40% in pre-market trading, opening around $90 and closing at $82.31 (a 26% gain but still 13% below the $95 offer, reflecting regulatory uncertainty). MSFT's stock dipped about 2.4%, closing lower due to the high cost and potential risks.

* **During Regulatory Review (2022-2023)**: ATVI's price fluctuated between $70 and $85, often trading at a discount to $95 amid antitrust challenges and delays. Market capitalization, or market cap, is the total value of all a company's outstanding shares of stock. It gives a bigger picture of how much the entire company is worth in the eyes of the market.

* **Why does it matter?** Market cap helps classify companies: small-cap (under $2 billion), mid-cap ($2-10 billion), large-cap (over $10 billion), and mega-cap (over $200 billion). It's useful for comparing companies and understanding their scale.

* **How to Calculate EPS**

The formula for diluted EPS is: **EPS = (Net Income - Preferred Dividends) ÷ Average Diluted Shares -Outstanding**

* **Net Income:** The company's total profit after expenses, taxes, and interest (often for the trailing twelve months, or TTM, for a recent snapshot). "Net Income Applicable to Common Shares" is used, subtracting any preferred dividends.

* **Average Diluted Shares Outstanding:** The weighted average number of shares during the period, including potential dilutive securities.

* **Simple analogy:** If a bakery earns $100 in profit (after preferred claims) and has 10 shares, each share gets $10 in earnings (EPS = 100 ÷ 10).

* **Important note:** Companies report this in financial statements; the calculation accounts for adjustments, so slight discrepancies may occur when verifying manually.

* **How to Calculate Dividend Yield**

The formula is:

**Dividend Yield (TTM)= (Past 12 Month's total Dividend per Share ÷ Current Stock Price) × 100%**

**Dividend Yield (Forward)= (Expected next year's total Dividend per Share ÷ Current Stock Price) × 100%**

**Annual Dividend per Share (Forward):** The total dividends expected or paid per share over a year (the forward estimate for the next 12 months).

**Annual Dividend per Share (TTM):** The total dividends expected or paid per share over a year (total dividend for the past 12 months).

**Current Stock Price:** The most recent trading price.

**Simple analogy:** If a stock is priced at $100 and pays $4 in annual dividends, the yield is 4%—meaning a 4% return from dividends alone.

#### **Something Practical about PE ratio and PB ratio:**

* **Why Some Companies Have High P/E Ratios While Others Have Low Ones:**

The price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio measures a company’s current share price relative to its earnings per share (EPS), reflecting how much investors are willing to pay for each dollar of the company’s earnings. The divergence between high and low P/E ratios stems from multiple factors related to a company’s performance, industry dynamics, and market expectations. Here is a detailed breakdown:

* **Reasons for High P/E Ratios**

  * **Strong Growth Expectations**

  This is the most common driver of high P/E ratios. Investors are willing to pay a premium for companies expected to deliver above-average earnings growth in the future. For example, startups or firms in emerging sectors (e.g., artificial intelligence, renewable energy tech) often have minimal current earnings but promising pipelines of products or services. The market bets on their ability to scale rapidly and generate substantial profits later, pushing up share prices relative to current EPS.

  * **Favorable Industry Traits**

  Companies in high-growth, asset-light industries (e.g., software, digital services) typically have higher P/E ratios than those in capital-intensive, mature sectors. These industries usually feature high profit margins, low production costs, and scalable business models, which support sustained earnings growth. In contrast, capital-heavy industries face higher operational costs and slower expansion potential, limiting their P/E upside.

  * **Low Dividend Payout Policies**

  Firms that reinvest most of their earnings into research and development (R\&D), expansion, or acquisitions instead of paying dividends tend to have higher P/E ratios. Investors value this reinvestment strategy because it signals the company’s focus on long-term growth, which is expected to drive future earnings and share price appreciation.

  * **Positive Market Sentiment and Speculation**

  In bull markets or for companies at the center of market trends (e.g., a social media platform during a user boom), investor enthusiasm and speculative buying can inflate share prices beyond what current earnings justify. This hype-driven demand leads to elevated P/E ratios, even if the company’s fundamentals do not fully support the valuation.

* **Reasons for Low P/E Ratios:**

  * **Mature or Declining Industry Position:**

  Companies in mature, slow-growth industries (e.g., utilities, traditional manufacturing) often have low P/E ratios. Their earnings are stable but lack significant growth potential, as the market is saturated, and competition is intense. Investors have limited expectations for future earnings growth, so they are unwilling to pay a high premium for current earnings.

  * **Cyclical Business Nature:**

  Cyclical companies (e.g., steel, automotive, construction) experience earnings fluctuations tied to economic cycles. During economic booms, their earnings surge, which lowers the P/E ratio (since EPS rises while share prices may not keep pace immediately). Conversely, in downturns, earnings drop, but P/E ratios may not rise sharply if investors anticipate a prolonged slump.

  * **Perceived Risks or Operational Challenges**

  A low P/E ratio can reflect investor concerns about a company’s prospects, such as poor management decisions, high debt levels, legal issues, or declining market share. These risks make investors cautious, leading them to demand a lower share price relative to earnings, resulting in a compressed P/E ratio.

  * **High Dividend Payouts:**

  Some companies, especially in mature sectors like utilities or consumer staples, distribute a large portion of their earnings as dividends. While dividends attract income-focused investors, the lack of reinvestment for growth means the company’s earnings potential is limited. Thus, the market assigns a lower P/E ratio, as investors prioritize steady dividend income over capital gains.

* **Why Some Companies Have High P/B Ratios While Others Have Low Ones:**

The price-to-book (P/B) ratio compares a company’s market capitalization to its book value of equity (total assets minus total liabilities, divided by the number of outstanding shares). It reflects how much investors are willing to pay for each dollar of a company’s net assets. The gap between high and low P/B ratios mainly depends on the quality of a company’s assets, its profitability, and market expectations for future returns. Here is a detailed explanation:

* **Reasons for High P/B Ratios:**

  * **Strong Asset Profitability (High ROE)&#x20;**

  The most critical driver of a high P/B ratio is a company’s ability to generate substantial profits from its net assets, measured by return on equity (ROE). Companies with high ROE—such as those in software, digital services, or luxury goods—often operate with asset-light business models. Their book value may be relatively low (e.g., minimal physical assets like factories), but they can leverage intangible resources (e.g., software algorithms, brand loyalty) to earn far above-average returns. Investors are willing to pay a premium for these efficient assets, pushing up the P/B ratio.

  * **Undervalued Intangible Assets**

  Book value is based on historical cost accounting, which often understates the value of intangible assets that are not fully recorded on the balance sheet. For example: Tech firms with valuable patents, proprietary technology, or strong customer bases. Luxury brands with high brand recognition and customer loyalty.

  These intangibles are critical to revenue and profit growth but may not be reflected in the book value. The market recognizes their true worth, leading to a higher market price relative to the stated book value, hence a high P/B ratio.

  * **Favorable Industry Prospects and Competitive Moats**

  Companies in high-growth industries (e.g., artificial intelligence, renewable energy) or those with strong competitive moats (e.g., network effects, regulatory barriers) tend to have high P/B ratios. Investors anticipate that these companies will continue to grow and maintain high profitability, so they are willing to pay more for their net assets. For instance, a leading cloud computing firm may have a high P/B ratio because its scalable platform and market dominance create sustainable value beyond its recorded book value.

  * **Positive Market Sentiment**

  In bull markets or during periods of sector-specific hype, investor enthusiasm can inflate share prices independently of book value. Speculative buying driven by optimism about future growth can push P/B ratios to elevated levels, even if a company’s current asset profitability is moderate.

* **Reasons for Low P/B Ratios**

  * **Weak Asset Profitability (Low ROE)**

  Companies with low ROE struggle to generate meaningful profits from their net assets, which leads to low P/B ratios. This is common in capital-intensive, mature industries like traditional manufacturing, steel production, or coal mining. These firms require massive investments in physical assets (factories, machinery, mines), which inflate their book value. However, due to overcapacity, low profit margins, or slow demand growth, their ROE remains low. Investors see little value in these underperforming assets, so they are unwilling to pay a premium over book value.

  * **Poor Asset Quality or Depreciating Assets**

  A low P/B ratio can signal that a company’s book value is inflated by low-quality or depreciating assets. For example: A manufacturing company with outdated machinery that has little resale value. A retail firm with excess inventory or unoccupied store locations that are losing value. In such cases, the book value on the balance sheet does not reflect the actual market value of the assets. Investors discount the stock price to account for this gap, resulting in a low P/B ratio.

  * **Cyclical Industry Downturns**

  Cyclical companies (e.g., automotive, construction, commodities) experience sharp swings in earnings tied to economic cycles. During a downturn, demand for their products collapses, leading to falling revenues and asset write-downs. Even if book value remains stable in the short term, investors anticipate further declines in asset value and profitability, so they sell the stock, pushing the P/B ratio down. In some cases, P/B ratios may drop below 1, meaning the market values the company at less than its stated net assets.

  * **High Financial Risk or Operational Distress**

  Companies burdened with high debt, legal issues, or declining market share often have low P/B ratios. High debt increases financial risk and reduces the value of equity (since liabilities are subtracted from assets to calculate book value). Operational distress—such as shrinking sales or management missteps—erodes investor confidence, leading to a lower stock price relative to book value.

  In summary, the P/B ratio is a gauge of asset quality and profitability: high P/B ratios indicate that investors believe a company’s assets can generate strong future returns, while low P/B ratios reflect weak asset profitability, poor asset quality, or market pessimism.

**An Exchange-Traded Fund (ETF) is an investment fund that holds a diversified basket of assets, such as stocks, bonds, commodities, or other securities, and trades on stock exchanges like individual stocks.** ETFs are designed to track the performance of a specific index, sector, commodity, or theme, providing investors with broad market exposure without needing to buy each underlying asset individually. Unlike mutual funds, ETFs can be bought and sold throughout the trading day at market prices, offering liquidity and flexibility.

## 3.1 ETF的主要特征（**Key characteristics**）

* **Pooled Investments**: ETFs pool money from multiple investors to purchase a portfolio of assets.

* **Exchange-Traded**: Traded on major exchanges (e.g., NYSE, NASDAQ) with real-time pricing.

* **Passive vs. Active**: Most are passive (index-tracking), but active ETFs, managed by professionals, are growing rapidly.

## 3.2 美国ETF发展史（History of ETFs in the U.S.）

ETFs originated in the U.S. in 1993 with the launch of the SPDR S\&P 500 ETF Trust (SPY), the first ETF, which tracked the S\&P 500 index. This innovation combined the diversification of mutual funds with the tradability of stocks. The market grew slowly initially but exploded in the 2000s due to regulatory changes and investor demand for low-cost, efficient vehicles.

* **Milestones:**

* **2000s**: Introduction of sector, international, and bond ETFs.

* **2010s**: Rise of smart-beta and thematic ETFs; AUM surpassed $1 trillion by 2010.

* **2020s**: Surge in active ETFs; by 2025, active ETFs represent 54% of all U.S.-listed ETFs, with AUM exceeding $1.17 trillion in mid-2025. Total ETFs launched in 2025: 1,167, a record.

## 3.3 ETF的运作原理（**How ETFs Work**）

ETFs operate through a creation/redemption mechanism involving authorized participants (APs), typically large institutions. APs create new ETF shares by delivering a basket of underlying securities to the ETF provider and redeem shares by exchanging them for the underlying assets. This process keeps the ETF's market price close to its net asset value (NAV), minimizing premiums or discounts.

* **Pricing**: Intraday trading at market prices; NAV calculated daily.

* **Expenses**: Low expense ratios (average around 0.4% for passive ETFs) due to passive management.

* **Tax Efficiency**: In-kind redemptions reduce capital gains distributions compared to mutual funds.

#### **Deep Analysis: Why Leveraged ETFs Are Not Suitable for Long-Term Holding:**

Leveraged ETFs (Exchange-Traded Funds) are financial instruments designed to deliver amplified daily returns based on an underlying index or asset. For example, a 2x leveraged ETF aims to provide twice the daily performance of its benchmark, while a 3x version targets three times. These products use derivatives like swaps, futures, and options to achieve this leverage, and they reset their exposure daily to maintain the target multiple. While they can be powerful tools for short-term trading or hedging, they are generally unsuitable for long-term holding due to a phenomenon known as **volatility decay** (also called volatility drag or beta slippage). This decay erodes the fund's value over time, especially in volatile or sideways markets, leading to returns that are significantly lower than what an investor might naively expect from simply multiplying the underlying asset's long-term return by the leverage factor.

In this analysis, I'll break down the concept of volatility decay step-by-step, explain its mathematical underpinnings, illustrate it with examples (including a simulated scenario), discuss its long-term implications, and touch on when leveraged ETFs might still be appropriate. The focus will be on how daily compounding and market fluctuations create this structural drag, making these ETFs a poor choice for buy-and-hold strategies.

1. **Understanding Leveraged ETFs and Their Daily Reset Mechanism**

To grasp volatility decay, it's essential to understand how leveraged ETFs operate. Unlike traditional ETFs, which track an index passively over time, leveraged ETFs are engineered for **daily** performance. Each day, the fund adjusts its holdings to ensure the next day's return is approximately the leverage multiple (e.g., 2x) of the underlying index's return, minus fees and expenses.

* **Daily Reset Example:** If the underlying index rises 1% on Day 1, a 2x leveraged ETF should rise about 2%. If the index then falls 1% on Day 2, the ETF falls about 2%. This reset happens every trading day, leading to compounding effects over multiple periods.

* **Key Insight:&#x20;**&#x54;he ETF's performance is path-dependent. It doesn't aim to deliver 2x the return over a week, month, or year—only over each single day. Over longer horizons, the cumulative return deviates from the simple multiple due to the interaction between leverage and volatility.

  This daily reset is the root cause of volatility decay. In a perfectly linear, upward-trending market with no volatility, a leveraged ETF would perform as expected (e.g., 2x the long-term return). However, real markets are volatile, and this volatility introduces a mathematical drag that compounds negatively over time.

2. **The Concept of Volatility Decay**

Volatility decay occurs because leveraged returns amplify both gains *and* losses, but losses have a disproportionately larger impact due to the nature of percentage-based compounding. In mathematical terms, when an asset experiences volatility (ups and downs), the geometric mean return (which determines compounded growth) is always less than or equal to the arithmetic mean return, with the difference increasing as volatility rises. Leverage magnifies this gap.

* **Core Mechanism:** Consider a simple two-day cycle where the underlying index goes up by *x*% and then down by approximately *x*% to return to its starting point. The underlying ends flat, but the leveraged ETF ends lower because the amplified loss on the down day is calculated on a higher base (after the up day).

* **Why It Happens:&#x20;**&#x47;ains and losses are not symmetric in percentage terms. To recover from a loss, you need a larger percentage gain. Leverage exacerbates this asymmetry.

#### **Leveraged ETFs with Built-In Strategies**

Leveraged ETFs are exchange-traded funds designed to amplify the daily returns of an underlying index, asset, or sector by a multiple (e.g., 2x or 3x) using derivatives like futures, swaps, or options. While traditional leveraged ETFs focus solely on this fixed daily leverage with a reset mechanism, those with **built-in strategies** incorporate additional layers of active or rules-based management to enhance performance, manage risk, or generate extra income. These strategies can include trend-following, volatility targeting, options overlays (e.g., covered calls), AI-driven adjustments, or dynamic allocation rules. The goal is often to mitigate issues like volatility decay (the erosion of returns over time due to compounding in volatile markets) or to adapt to changing market conditions, making them potentially more suitable for tactical or longer-term use compared to plain-vanilla leveraged products.

These ETFs are typically aimed at experienced traders or investors seeking amplified exposure with some embedded intelligence, but they still carry high risks due to leverage, including magnified losses, higher fees (often 0.9-1.5%), and path dependency. They are not meant for passive, long-term buy-and-hold without monitoring, as the built-in strategies may not always outperform in all environments.

#### **Key Features of Built-In Strategies**

* **Trend-Following or Timing**: Some ETFs use signals like moving averages (e.g., 200-day SMA) to adjust leverage exposure, reducing it during downtrends to avoid prolonged decay. For example, strategies might rotate between leveraged positions and cash or unleveraged assets based on market momentum.

* **Options-Based Overlays**: These incorporate selling options (e.g., covered calls) on top of the leveraged base to generate yield or buffer downside. This can provide income boosts but may cap upside potential.

* **Diversification or Hedging**: Built-in rules might blend leveraged equities with bonds, commodities, or inverse positions for risk parity, rebalancing periodically to harvest volatility premiums.

## 4.1 看涨期权与看跌期权（What Are Call and Put Options and Option）

**Options are contracts that give the buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an underlying asset at a predetermined price (the exercise or strike price) by a specific date (expiration).** The underlying is often a stock, like AAPL. The buyer pays a premium to the seller for this right. Options are contingent claims, meaning the payoff depends on whether exercising the option is beneficial at expiration.

### 4.1.1 关键项目 Key items you need to know first

* **Strike Price**: This is the fixed price at which you can buy (for a call option) or sell (for a put option) the underlying asset, like a stock, if you choose to exercise the option. It's set when you buy the option and doesn't change.&#x20;

* **Premium**: This is the cost you pay upfront to buy the option contract. It's like the price of the option itself, determined by factors such as the stock's current price, time until expiration, and market volatility.&#x20;

* **Exercise Date**: This refers to the date on which you can actually use (or "exercise") the option to buy or sell the underlying asset at the strike price. For most options, it's the same as the expiration date, but some (like American-style options) allow exercise anytime before that date.

1. **Call Options (Long Position - Buyer)**

A call option gives the buyer the right to buy the underlying at the strike price. Buyers use calls when they expect the underlying price to rise. The payoff at expiration is max (0, S\_T - X), where S\_T is the underlying price at expiration and X is the strike.

If S\_T > X, the buyer exercises and profits from the difference (minus the premium paid).

If S\_T ≤ X, the option expires worthless, and the loss is limited to the premium.

Payoff Profile for a Long Call (Buyer): The profile starts at (-premium) below the strike, then rises linearly above X with unlimited upside.

Breakeven Point for a Long Call:&#x20;

For a long call option, the breakeven point at expiration is the stock price at which the option's payoff exactly offsets the premium paid, resulting in zero profit or loss.

The formula for the breakeven stock price on a call is: Breakeven = Strike Price + Premium Paid.

To calculate it:

1. Identify the strike price.

2. Add the premium (cost) you paid for the call option, the result is the breakeven price

Real Market Example: As of January 15, 2026, AAPL trades at $258.41. A January 16, 2026 call with strike $250 last traded at $8.50. If AAPL closes at $270 at expiration, the payoff is $270 - $250 = $20, for a profit of $20 - $8.50 = $11.50 per share (options cover 100 shares). If AAPL closes at $240, the option expires worthless, and the loss is the $8.50($850 for 100 shares) premium. Breakeven price = $250 + $8.5 = $258.5

* **Put Options (Long Position - Buyer)**

A put option gives the buyer the right to sell the underlying at the strike price. Buyers use puts when they expect the underlying price to fall. The payoff at expiration is max (0, X - S\_T).

If S\_T < X, the buyer exercises and profits from the difference (minus the premium).

If S\_T ≥ X, the option expires worthless, and the loss is limited to the premium.

Payoff Profile for a Long Put (Buyer): The profile rises linearly below X up to the premium amount, then flattens at -premium above X.

For a long put option (buying a put), the breakeven point at expiration is the stock price at which the option's payoff exactly offsets the premium paid, resulting in zero profit or loss.

Breakeven Point for a Long Put:&#x20;

The formula for the breakeven stock price on a long put is: Breakeven = Strike Price - Premium Paid.

To calculate it:

1. Identify the strike price.

2. Subtract the premium (cost) you paid for the put option. The result is the breakeven price.

### 4.1.2 **期权的盈利性 Option Moneyness**

Options are classified based on the relationship between the current underlying price (S) and the strike price (X). This moneyness affects the option's intrinsic value (immediate exercise value) and its premium.

* **Quick Note on Option Time Value:&#x20;**&#x54;he "time value" in options is the part of the premium beyond the intrinsic value—it's the extra cost buyers pay for the chance that the underlying price might move favorably before expiration. Think of it as paying for "hope" or potential upside over time. Time value decreases as expiration approaches (known as time decay or theta), and it's zero at expiration when only intrinsic value remains

* **Quick Note on Intrinsic value:&#x20;**&#x49;ntrinsic value is the actual, built-in profit (or "real" value) of an option if you were to exercise it right now, based on the current price of the underlying stock compared to the strike price.

In-the-Money (ITM): The option has positive intrinsic value. For a call, S > X (intrinsic value = S - X). For a put, S < X (intrinsic value = X - S). ITM options are more likely to be exercised and have higher premiums due to built-in profit.

At-the-Money (ATM): S ≈ X, intrinsic value is zero or near zero. Premium consists mostly of time value.

Out-of-the-Money (OTM): The option has no intrinsic value. For a call, S < X. For a put, S > X. Premium is purely time value, reflecting potential for future movement.

Real Market Examples (Take Apple as example, January 16, 2026 expiration option, today is January 15, 2026, current Apple price at $258.21):

Call ITM: Strike $250 (S = $258.21 > $250), intrinsic value $8.21, premium $8.50 (includes slight time value)

Call ATM: Strike $260 (S ≈ $260), intrinsic value ≈$0, premium $0.66 (All from time value)

Call OTM: Strike $265 (S < $265), intrinsic value $0, premium $0.06 (All from time value)

Put ITM: Strike $262.5 (S < $260), intrinsic value $4.29, premium $4.47 (includes some time value)

Put ATM: Strike $257.5 (S ≈ $257.5), intrinsic value ≈$0, premium $0.95 (All from time value)

Put OTM: Strike $255 (S > $255), intrinsic value $0, premium $0.33 (All from time value)

### 4.1.3 **美国vs.欧洲期权 American vs. European Options**

1. **What Are American Options?**

American options give the buyer the right to exercise the contract at any time before or on the expiration date. This flexibility allows investors to act on favorable market moves early, but it often comes with higher premiums due to the added value. For example, if you hold an American call on AAPL and the stock surges mid-contract, you can exercise immediately to buy shares at the strike price.

* **What Are European Options?**

European options can only be exercised on the expiration date, not before. This limits timing flexibility but typically results in lower premiums, as there's no early exercise risk for sellers. They're simpler for pricing models like Black-Scholes, which assume no early exercise.

* **Key Differences**

**Exercise Timing:** American: Anytime up to expiration; European: Only at expiration.

**Premiums:** American options usually cost more due to flexibility.

**Usage:** American suit active strategies; European are common for indices to avoid disruptions.

* **In the US Stock Market**

  * Most US equity options (on individual stocks and ETFs like AAPL or SPY) are American style, allowing early exercise. Index options (e.g., SPX on S\&P 500) are typically European style. Main underlying assets include: Individual stocks (e.g., AAPL, TSLA); ETFs (e.g., SPY, QQQ); Indices (e.g., S\&P 500, Nasdaq-100); Some commodities and futures, but equities dominate retail trading.

* **In the Hong Kong Stock Market**

  * HKEX stock options (on individual stocks) are American-style, exercisable anytime up to expiration. Index options (e.g., Hang Seng Index) are European-style, exercisable only at expiration. Main underlying assets include: Individual stocks (selected blue-chips like Tencent, HSBC); Indices (e.g., Hang Seng Index (HSI), HSCEI, Hang Seng TECH Index); some currencies and commodities, but stocks and indices are primary.

## 4.3 <span style="color: inherit; background-color: rgba(254,212,164,0.8)">期权定价模型 Introduction to the Black-Scholes Option Pricing Model (Will not be tested)-【不纳入1星考试】</span>

The Black-Scholes (BS) model is a widely used mathematical framework for estimating the fair price of European-style options (options that can only be exercised at expiration). Developed in 1973 by Fischer Black and Myron Scholes, it provides a theoretical value based on several key assumptions, such as no dividends on the underlying stock, constant volatility, and efficient markets. While the full model involves advanced math, you don't need to master the calculations—think of it as a tool that helps explain why option prices change with market conditions.

### 4.3.2 要点 Main Points

* **Assumptions**: The model assumes markets are frictionless (no transaction costs), the risk-free rate and volatility are constant, the underlying follows a lognormal distribution (prices can't go negative), and no arbitrage opportunities exist.

* **Limitations**: In reality, volatility isn't constant, stocks pay dividends, and early exercise (for American options) isn't accounted for. It's a starting point, not perfect.

* **Uses**: Helps in pricing options, assessing if they're over- or under-valued, and understanding sensitivities (via Greeks like delta, gamma, theta, vega—see Section 4).

### 4.4.3 末日期权（**Zero Days to Expiration (0DTE) Options（Source From Schwab and MooMoo, Not From CFA, Just need to know basic information)**）

Zero Days to Expiration (0DTE) options are options contracts that expire at the end of the same trading day they are purchased or sold. Introduced broadly in 2022 by exchanges like the Cboe, they have exploded in popularity, now accounting for about 50% of daily S\&P 500 index options volume. These are typically available on major indices and ETFs, such as SPX (S\&P 500), SPY (S\&P 500 ETF), NDX (Nasdaq-100), and QQQ (Nasdaq-100 ETF), with expirations offered on specific days like Mondays, Wednesdays, and Fridays for SPX, and daily for SPY and QQQ. Unlike longer-dated options, 0DTEs have no time value beyond the current session, making them hyper-sensitive to immediate price moves.

* **Call Options in 0DTE**

A 0DTE call gives the buyer the right to buy the underlying at the strike price by day's end. Payoff: max (0, S\_T - X), where S\_T is the closing price.

If ITM (S\_T > X): Exercise for intrinsic value minus premium; profits from quick upside spikes.

If ATM/OTM (S\_T ≤ X): Expires worthless; full premium lost.

For sellers: Collect premium but risk assignment if ITM, with unlimited upside loss potential in volatile markets.

* **Real Market Example:** With SPY at $550 intraday, buy a 0DTE $552 call for $0.50 premium. If SPY closes at $555, payoff $3, net profit $2.50. If below $552, lose $0.50 fully.

* **Put Options in 0DTE**

A 0DTE put allows selling the underlying at the strike by close. Payoff: max(0, X - S\_T).

If ITM (S\_T < X): Exercise for value minus premium; gains from rapid drops.

If ATM/OTM (S\_T ≥ X): Worthless; premium gone.

Sellers: Keep premium if unexercised, but must buy at strike if assigned, risking down to zero.

* **Real Market Example:** SPY at $550, buy 0DTE $548 put for $0.40. If closes at $545, payoff $3, net $2.60. If above $548, lose $0.40.

* **Settlement and Key Points**

  * **Trading Hours:** Trade until 4:00 PM ET; no after-hours for 0DTEs. Settlement uses closing price.

  * **Type:** Mostly cash-settled for indices (SPX/NDX), physical for ETFs (SPY/QQQ).

  * **Auto-Exercise:** ITM by $0.01+ auto-exercised; OTM expire automatically.

  * **Greeks Impact:** Extreme theta decay (value erodes rapidly) and high gamma (sensitive to small moves), ideal for day trading but punishing for holders.

* **Risks**

> **High Volatility:** Prices can swing wildly; 90%+ expire worthless, per studies.
>
> **Leverage Amplifies Losses:** Low premiums mean cheap entry, but total loss is common; no time for recovery.
>
> **Assignment/Pin Risk:** Near strikes at close can lead to unexpected assignment or gaps.

* **Market Impact:**&#x20;

Heavy 0DTE volume can cause intraday volatility spikes or "gamma squeezes."

* **Market Environment：**

0DTEs thrive in volatile sessions with news events (e.g., earnings, Fed announcements) for directional bets, or range-bound days for selling premium. Popular among retail for low-cost speculation but suit experienced day traders avoiding over-leverage.&#x20;

**Introduction to Wealth Management Products**

This training material provides a basic overview of key investment vehicles in the US market: money market funds, mutual funds, hybrid (or balanced) funds, and private markets (including private equity and venture capital). The content is primarily based on the CFA Level 1 Curriculum, which covers aspects of the asset management industry and pooled investment products.

## 5.1 **资产管理行业概述 Overview of the Asset Management Industry**

The asset management industry manages investments for individuals and institutions, helping them grow wealth while managing risk. As of 2017 (from the curriculum), global AUM was over $79 trillion, with nearly 80% in North America and Europe. By 2025, this has grown to about $110 trillion globally, with the US alone accounting for around $50 trillion.

Key aspects from the curriculum:

* **Industry Structure**: Includes "pure-play" asset managers (e.g., Vanguard, BlackRock) and diversified firms like banks or insurers that offer investment services.

* **Global Reach**: Many firms have offices worldwide to handle international investments.

* **Competition**: The industry is highly competitive, focusing on performance, fees, and client service.

* **Supplement for Novices**: Asset managers charge fees (e.g., 0.5%-1% of AUM annually) for their services. In the US, they must register with the SEC if managing over $100 million in assets, ensuring transparency and investor protection.

## 5.2 **主动与被动管理 Active vs. Passive Management**

The curriculum distinguishes two main approaches to managing investments:

* **Active Management**: Managers actively select securities to outperform a benchmark (e.g., S\&P 500). They use research, forecasts, and timing to buy/sell. This is common in mutual funds aiming for higher returns but involves higher fees and risks if the manager underperforms.

* **Passive Management**: Managers track a market index (e.g., via ETFs like SPY). Lower fees, as there's no "picking" involved. The curriculum notes passive strategies have grown due to cost efficiency.

* **Supplement for Novices**: In the US, passive funds now hold over 50% of equity fund assets, active management can beat the market in volatile times but often lags after fees (source: Morningstar Active/Passive Barometer 2025). For clients, recommend passive for low-cost diversification and active for specialized goals.

## 5.3 **传统资产管理公司与另类资产管理公司 Traditional vs. Alternative Asset Managers**

* **Traditional Managers**: Focus on stocks, bonds, and cash. They manage mutual funds and aim for steady growth.

* **Alternative Managers**: Handle non-traditional assets like hedge funds, private equity, real estate, or commodities. These often seek higher returns but with more risk and less liquidity.

* **Insight**: Alternatives have grown due to low interest rates and the search for yield. In 2017, alternatives were about 15% of global AUM; by 2025, they've risen to 20%&#x20;

* **Supplement for Novices**: In the US, alternatives are for "accredited investors" (net worth >$1 million or income >$200k), per SEC rules, due to higher risks (source: SEC Regulation D).

## 5.4 **资产管理行业趋势 Asset Management Industry Trends**

**Key trends:**

* 1.Shift to passive investing.

* 2.Growth in alternatives for diversification.

* 3.Technology (e.g., robo-advisors) lowering costs.

* 4.Focus on ESG (environmental, social, governance) factors.

## 5.5 **集合投资工具 Pooled Investment Vehicles**

Taxable (corporate/gov't debt) or tax-free (muni debt). Daily liquidity; Not FDIC-insured but considered safe. The yield on the U.S. dollar money market funds generally tracks the short-term T-bill rates closely, which means the yield on money market funds generally goes up in the rate hike cycle and goes down during the cut cycle.

### 5.6.2 混合型/平衡型基金 Hybrid/Balanced Funds

**Operations:**&#x20;

A balanced fund is a combination of a growth fund and an income fund. Balanced funds invest in common stocks, preferred stocks, long-term bonds, and short-term bonds, aiming to provide both income and capital appreciation while minimizing risk. These funds don’t get hammered too badly when the market is bearish but usually underperform when the market is bullish.&#x20;

**Investment Points:**

* **Benefits:** Built-in diversification, moderate risk/return; automatic rebalancing.

* **Risks:** Equity volatility, interest rate sensitivity; fees higher than pure index funds.

* **Suitability:** Moderate-risk investors, retirement planning; simplifies allocation.

* **Example:** Vanguard Balanced Index Fund (VBALX) – 60% stocks/40% bonds. AUM \~$50B in 2025; historical 7-8% annual returns, 2025 return \~12% amid stock gains.

### 5.6.3 私募市场（私募股权和风险投资）Private Markets (Private Equity and Venture Capital)

**Operations:** Funds buy private companies (not public), optimize (e.g., management changes, synergies), exit via sale/IPO after 5-10 years. Structured as limited partnerships: GP (manager) handles investments; LPs (investors) commit capital. Fees: 1-3% management + 20% carried interest (profits after return hurdle). Illiquid; lockups 7-10 years, periodic distributions.

### 5.6.4 **收益型基金 Income fund**

The primary objective of an income fund is to provide current revenue (not growth) for investors. This type of fund invests most of its assets in a diversified portfolio of (a) debt securities that pay interest and in preferred and (b) common stock of companies that are known to pay consistent dividends in cash.&#x20;

### 5.6.5 **增长型基金Growth fund**

Growth funds invest most of their assets in a diversified portfolio of the common stock of relatively new and existing companies, looking for big increases in the stock prices. Growth funds offer a higher potential for growth but usually at a higher risk for the investor. This type of fund is ideal for an investor who’s looking for long-term capital appreciation potential.&#x20;

Because of the inherent risk of investing in growth funds, they’re better for younger investors who can take the risk because they have more time to recover their losses.&#x20;

Some growth funds are labeled as *aggressive growth funds&#x20;*&#x62;ecause the securities it invests in are even riskier than those of a standard growth fund.&#x20;

### 5.6.6&#x20;**&#x20;专业（行业）基金 Specialized (sector) fund**

A specialized or sector fund is a type of fund that invests primarily in the securities of a single industry. A specialized fund may invest only in financial services, healthcare, automotive stocks, technology securities, and so on. Because specialized funds are limited in their investments, you can assume that in many cases, they’re a little riskier (more volatile) than the average fund.&#x20;

### 5.6.7&#x20;**&#x20;国际或全球基金 International or global fund**

An international fund invests in companies based anywhere outside of the investor’s home country. A global fund invests in securities located anywhere in the world, including the investor’s home country. Although international and global funds may be good to round out a portfolio, they aren’t without their risks. Along with the risk that investors face by just investing in securities in general, holders of international and global funds also face currency risk, which is the risk that the currency exchange rate between the U.S. and foreign issuers will hurt investors. There’s also the additional risk that politics in a particular country will harm the value of the fund.

### 5.6.8 **指数基金 Index fund**

This type of fund invests in securities that are similar to a particular stock or bond index. Rather than being actively managed, these types of funds are passively managed, which means investors can expect lower or no management fees. Investors can expect that the NAV of the fund will increase or decrease based on the movement of the benchmark index (S\&P 500, DJIA, and so on) the fund mimics.&#x20;

### 5.6.9 **对冲基金 Hedge funds**

Hedge funds hold a pool of investments and are professionally managed like mutual (open-end) funds but have a lot more flexibility. Hedge funds are typically much more aggressive in nature and may buy securities on margin, sell securities short, purchase or sell options, and so on in an attempt to maximize gains.

## 5.7 **了解主要基金费用 Understanding Key Fund Fees**

### 5.7.1 **前端收费（销售负担） Class A: Front-End Load (Sales Load)**

* **What it is:** A one-time sales charge paid when you buy shares in the fund. It's a percentage of your investment. The investor pays the load when purchasing shares of the fund. These funds typically are better for long-term investors because they usually have lower expense ratios, and they have breakpoints for large dollar purchases. For example, if you invest $10,000 in a fund with a 1% front-end load, $100 goes to the fee, and only $9,900 is invested.

* **What it means:** This fee compensates brokers or salespeople for selling the fund. It reduces the amount of money that starts working for you right away.

* **Applicable products:** Primarily mutual funds (load funds). Not common in money market funds, ETFs, or private markets. Many funds are now "no-load," meaning no front-end charge.

### 5.7.2 **后端收费 （递延销售费用）Class B: Back-End Load (Deferred Sales Charge)**

* **What it is:&#x20;**&#x41; sales charge paid when you sell or redeem shares. It often decreases over time. Also called contingent deferred sales charge. The investor pays the load when redeeming shares of the fund. These funds have higher expense ratios than Class A but often convert to Class A if held for many years. Very few mutual fund companies offer Class B shares at this point.&#x20;

* **What it means:** Encourages long-term holding; the fee covers distribution costs if you exit early.

* **Applicable products:** Some mutual funds (load funds). Rare in other products like ETFs or private funds.

### 5.7.3 **均衡佣金 Class C: level load**

The investors pay a periodic fee (usually, annually) over the time period that they hold the fund. These funds have higher expense ratios than Class A and have an exit fee, which is often eliminated after a year or two. These funds typically are the best option for short-term investors.&#x20;

### 5.7.4 **免佣基金 Class D: No-load**

Investors don’t pay a sales charge but may be charged some sort of transaction fee. Unlike load mutual funds, no-load funds may not charge a 12b-1 fee greater than 25 basis points.&#x20;

### 5.7.5 **管理费 Management Fee**

* **What it is**: An annual fee charged as a percentage of the fund's assets under management (AUM). For example, 1% on $100,000 means $1,000 per year.

* **What it means**: Pays the fund manager for selecting investments, research, and day-to-day operations. It's part of the overall expense ratio and deducted automatically from the fund's returns.

* **Applicable products**: All funds, including mutual funds, money market funds, hybrid funds, ETFs, hedge funds, and private equity. In hedge funds, it's often 2%; in private equity, 1-3% of committed capital.

### 5.7.6 **绩效费（激励费用或附带权益费） Performance Fee (Incentive Fee or Carried Interest)**

* **What it is**: A fee based on the fund's profits, typically 20% of gains above a certain threshold (hurdle rate, e.g., 8% return). Often includes a "high-water mark" – no fee until the fund recovers from losses.

* **What it means**: Aligns the manager's incentives with investors; they earn more only if the fund performs well. In private equity, it's called "carried interest" and paid on profits from selling companies.

* **Applicable products**: Mainly alternative investments like hedge funds and private equity/venture capital funds. Not typical in mutual funds, money market funds, or hybrid funds, which focus on management fees.

## 6.1 **如何计算指数收益（两种关键类型）How to Calculate Index Returns (Two Key Types)**

Indexes track the "average performance" of a group of stocks (e.g., S\&P 500 tracks 500 large U.S. companies). Returns measure how much the index has gone up/down.

* **Price Return**: Price return only looks at changes in the index level itself, ignoring any dividends paid by the companies in the index. It shows pure price appreciation (or depreciation).&#x20;

  * Price Return = \[(Ending Index Level - Beginning Index Level) / Beginning Index Level] × 100%

  * Example: For the S\&P 500 in 2025, it started at approximately 5,903 open) and ended at 6,845. Price Return = \[(6,845 - 5,903) / 5,903] × 100% = 15.96%. This means the index's price rose by about 16% due to stock price increases alone.

* **Total Return**: Total return includes both price changes and dividends paid (assuming they're reinvested back into the index). It gives a fuller picture of what an investor might actually earn.

  * Total Return = \[(Ending Index Value with Reinvested Dividends - Beginning Index Value) / Beginning Index Value] × 100%

  * Example: Using the same S\&P 500 for 2025, the total return (with dividends reinvested) was 17.9%. This is higher than the price return because dividends from S\&P 500 companies added extra gains when reinvested.&#x20;

## 7.1 **财务报表的目的/意义以及通用会计准则 The Purpose/significance of Financial Statements, and Common Accounting Standard**

Financial statements are formal records that summarize a company's financial activities over a specific period. They provide a structured overview of a firm's assets, liabilities, revenues, expenses, and cash flows, enabling stakeholders to assess its overall health and performance.

In the US stock market, public companies are required by the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) to file these statements regularly—typically quarterly (via Form 10-Q) and annually (via Form 10-K)—to promote transparency and protect investors. This regulatory mandate ensures that market participants have access to reliable data, reducing information asymmetry and supporting fair trading on exchanges like the NYSE or Nasdaq.

The core purpose is threefold:

* **Transparency for Investors**: Statements allow shareholders to evaluate how their capital is being used. For instance, they reveal profitability trends, debt levels, and operational efficiency, which inform decisions on buying, holding, or selling stocks.

* **Accountability for Management**: They hold executives responsible by documenting results against goals, often influencing corporate governance, such as board votes or executive compensation.

* **Market Efficiency**: By standardizing reporting, statements facilitate comparisons across companies, helping the market price stocks accurately based on fundamentals rather than speculation.

### 7.1.1 **财务报表分析的意义 The Significance of Financial Statement Analysis**

Analyzing financial statements involves reviewing and interpreting the data to draw conclusions about a company's viability, growth potential, and risks. This process is critical in financial markets because it translates raw numbers into actionable insights, directly influencing stock prices and investment strategies.

Key benefits include:

* **Informed Investment Decisions**: Analysis helps identify undervalued or overvalued stocks by examining metrics like earnings growth or debt ratios. It reveals whether a company can generate returns exceeding its cost of capital, a key factor in stock performance.

* **Risk Assessment**: It uncovers vulnerabilities, such as high leverage or declining cash flows, allowing investors to avoid potential losses during market downturns.

* **Performance Forecasting**: By spotting trends, analysts can predict future earnings, which drives buy/sell recommendations and impacts market sentiment.

### 7.1.2 **通用会计准则 Common Accounting Standards**

Financial statements are prepared under standardized rules to ensure consistency. The two most prevalent frameworks are US Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP) and International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS). While GAAP is mandatory for US public companies, IFRS is used by many international firms, including those with US listings via American Depositary Receipts (ADRs).

* **US GAAP**: Developed by the Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB), GAAP is rules-based, providing detailed guidelines for specific scenarios. It emphasizes precision and is tailored to the US legal environment, making it ideal for complex transactions in litigious markets.

* **IFRS**: Issued by the International Accounting Standards Board (IASB), IFRS is principles-based, offering broader guidance that requires professional judgment. It's adopted in over 140 countries, promoting global comparability but allowing more flexibility.

* **Applicable Regions**

US GAAP is predominantly applied in the United States, where it is mandated by the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) for all domestic public companies filing financial reports. This ensures uniformity in the US capital markets, facilitating investor comparisons and regulatory oversight. While some foreign entities may voluntarily adopt US GAAP for US listings or to align with American investors, it is not widely used outside the US. For instance, in 2026, the US remains the primary jurisdiction requiring GAAP, with no significant international adoptions beyond specific cross-border scenarios.

In contrast, IFRS is the dominant standard internationally, required for public companies in over 140 jurisdictions worldwide as of 2026. This includes all European Union member states (e.g., Germany, France), the United Kingdom, Australia, New Zealand, Canada (for publicly accountable enterprises), South Africa, Brazil, and South Korea. In Japan, IFRS is permitted for certain domestic companies and required for some consolidated financial statements, while countries like Mexico and Singapore have fully adopted it for listed entities. The widespread use of IFRS promotes cross-border investment by standardizing reporting, though it is not mandatory for US domestic firms—foreign private issuers listed on US exchanges can use IFRS without reconciliation to GAAP.

## 7.2 **损益表分析 Analyzing the Income Statement**

### 7.2.1 什么是**损益**表？它为何重要？What Is the Income Statement and Why Does It Matter?

The income statement, also known as the statement of operations, profit and loss statement, or P\&L, provides a summary of a company's revenues, expenses, gains, and losses over a defined period, such as a fiscal quarter or year. It calculates net income (or loss) by subtracting expenses from revenues, which represents the profit available to shareholders after all costs. This statement is accrual-based, meaning it records transactions when they occur economically, not necessarily when cash changes hands, allowing for a more accurate picture of performance.

In the US financial markets, the income statement is a core component of SEC filings like Form 10-Q (quarterly) and 10-K (annual). It directly influences stock valuations through metrics like earnings per share (EPS), which analysts use to compute price-to-earnings (P/E) ratios. For analysts, it's essential for assessing a company's profitability trends, operational efficiency, and growth potential. Strong earnings can fuel stock rallies, while misses often lead to sell-offs, impacting trading volumes and client portfolios.

Key significance:

* **Profitability Assessment**: Reveals how effectively a company converts revenues into profits, highlighting strengths (e.g., high margins) or weaknesses (e.g., rising costs).

* **Trend Analysis**: Year-over-year (YoY) or quarter-over-quarter (QoQ) comparisons show growth trajectories, such as expanding revenues or improving margins, which inform buy/sell recommendations.

* **Forecasting and Valuation**: Serves as the foundation for earnings models; analysts project future EPS to derive target prices using discounted cash flow (DCF) or multiples.

* **Market Impact**: Earnings releases based on this statement drive volatility—e.g., the NYSE and Nasdaq see average daily volume spikes of 20-30% during earnings seasons as investors react.

* **Risk Identification**: Flags issues like one-time charges or margin erosion, helping brokerages advise on hedging or diversification.

* **Regulatory Compliance**: Ensures transparency under SEC rules, preventing fraud like revenue inflation, which could lead to restatements and stock plunges.

## 7.3 分析资产负债表 Analyzing the Balance Sheet

### 7.3.1 什么是资产负债表及其重要性 What Is the Balance Sheet and Why Does It Matter?

The balance sheet, also known as the statement of financial position, provides a snapshot of a company's financial position at a specific point in time, typically the end of a fiscal quarter (Form 10-Q) or year (Form 10-K). It lists what the company owns (assets), what it owes (liabilities), and the residual interest of shareholders (equity), adhering to the fundamental accounting equation: **Assets = Liabilities + Equity**. Unlike the income statement, which covers a period, the balance sheet is point-in-time and accrual-based, reflecting historical costs adjusted for depreciation, impairments, and fair value where applicable.

In the US financial markets, the balance sheet is crucial for SEC filings, offering insights into a company's solvency, liquidity, and capital structure. It helps assess whether a firm can meet obligations, fund growth, or weather downturns, directly impacting stock valuations through ratios like debt-to-equity or return on assets (ROA). For analyst, it's vital for credit analysis (e.g., bond recommendations), M\&A due diligence, and risk management, as imbalances can signal distress or opportunities.

Key significance:

* **Solvency and Stability Evaluation**: Shows leverage and asset quality, revealing if a company is over-indebted or asset-rich, which affects credit ratings and borrowing costs.

* **Liquidity Assessment**: Current assets vs. liabilities indicate short-term health, critical during market stresses like recessions.

* **Investment and Valuation Insights**: Equity components track shareholder value creation; asset breakdowns highlight intangibles or investments driving multiples like price-to-book (P/B).

* **Trend and Comparative Analysis**: Period-over-period changes spot growth (e.g., asset expansion) or red flags (e.g., rising debt); common-size analysis normalizes for peer comparisons.

* **Regulatory and Compliance Role**: Ensures adherence to GAAP/IFRS, preventing manipulations like off-balance-sheet financing, which could lead to SEC probes and price drops.

## 7.4 **现金流量表 Cash Flow Statement**

### 7.4.1 什么是现金流量表？What Is the Statement of Cash Flows?

The statement of cash flows (also known as the cash flow statement) is a primary financial statement that provides information about a company's cash receipts and cash payments during an accounting period. It reconciles the beginning and ending cash balances on the balance sheet, classifying cash inflows and outflows into three categories: **operating activities&#x20;**(core business cash), **investing activities** (asset acquisitions/disposals), and **financing activities** (funding sources/returns). Unlike the income statement, which uses accrual accounting, the cash flow statement focuses on actual cash movements, helping assess liquidity, solvency, and financial flexibility. It is a "flow" statement, reporting performance over time, and is required under US GAAP and IFRS.

* **Cash Flows from Operating Activities**

Cash flows from operating activities (CFO or operating cash flow) is the section of the cash flow statement that reports net cash generated or used by a company's core business operations during the period, such as selling products or providing services. It represents the cash version of the income statement's operating profit, excluding non-cash items and timing differences.

* **Cash Flows from Investing Activities**

Cash flows from investing activities is the section of the cash flow statement that reports net cash used or generated from long-term asset transactions, such as buying or selling property, equipment, securities, or businesses, reflecting a company's capital allocation and growth strategies.

* **Cash Flows from Financing Activities**

Cash flows from financing activities is the section of the cash flow statement that reports net cash generated or used from transactions with creditors and owners, such as issuing or repaying debt, issuing or repurchasing stock, and paying dividends, reflecting how a company funds its operations and returns capital.

## 8.1 **卖方公司研究报告 Sell-Side Company Research Reports**

Sell-side analysts (from investment banks or brokerages) produce these reports to guide client investment decisions. They combine fundamental analysis with recommendations (e.g., Buy, Hold, Sell) and target prices.

* **Purpose**: Provide independent insights on company fundamentals, industry dynamics, and valuation. Support client portfolio decisions and generate trading activity.

* **Typical Structure and Key Content**:

1. **Executive Summary / Investment Thesis** — Concise overview of recommendation, target price, and rationale.

2. **Company Overview** — Business model, products/services, and strategy.

3. **Industry and Competitive Analysis** — Market trends, peers, and forces.

4. **Financial Review** — Historical results, revenue/profit drivers, margins.

5. **Forecasts and Valuation** — Revenue/earnings projections, DCF or multiples-based valuation.

6. **Risks and Catalysts** — Key risks and upside/downside scenarios.

7. **Recommendation and Target Price** — With upside/downside potential.

Example：GS's latest research report on Apple

![](<images/入门考试学习资料 Learning Resources for Entry Knowledge Test-image-98.png>)

Analyst Ratings (Buy, Hold, Sell): Analyst recommendations are a key feature of sell-side reports, typically expressed as one of three core ratings: Buy, Hold, or Sell. These are based on the analyst's assessment of the stock's expected performance relative to a benchmark (e.g., market index like S\&P 500) over a specified horizon (usually 12 months). Ratings are tied to the target price, which is derived from valuation models, and must include the firm's rating system definition for transparency.&#x20;

Buy (or Overweight/Outperform/Strong Buy): This rating indicates the analyst expects the stock to significantly outperform the market or its benchmark. Typically, this means anticipated total return (price appreciation + dividends) of 10–20% or more above the benchmark's expected return. For example, if the benchmark is projected to return 8%, a Buy might imply >18% total return for the stock. It signals strong conviction in upside drivers like revenue growth, margin expansion, or undervaluation. Investors are encouraged to purchase or add to positions. In reports, Buys are supported by bullish theses, such as a sustainable competitive moat or favorable industry trends.

Hold (or Neutral/Market Perform): A neutral stance where the analyst expects the stock to perform in line with the market or benchmark, with limited upside or downside (e.g., total return within ±5–10% of the benchmark). This rating suggests the stock is fairly valued at current levels, with balanced risks and rewards. It might be used when the company is stable but lacks catalysts for outperformance, or when awaiting more data (e.g., upcoming earnings). Investors are advised to maintain existing positions but not aggressively buy or sell. Holds are the most common rating, often reflecting caution or equilibrium.

Sell (or Underweight/Underperform/Strong Sell): This signals the analyst expects the stock to underperform the market or benchmark, often with significant downside (e.g., total return 10–20% or more below the benchmark). Reasons could include overvaluation, deteriorating fundamentals (e.g., weakening revenue drivers, competitive threats), or high risks. Investors are recommended to reduce or exit positions. Sells are rarer (often <10% of ratings due to potential backlash from covered companies), but they demonstrate independent analysis. Reports with Sells emphasize quantified risks and alternative investments.

## 8.2 **收入分析与收入驱动因素 Revenue Analysis and Revenue Drivers**

Revenue drivers are factors that causally influence revenue levels and growth. Analysts start by analyzing historical revenues to identify these drivers, then assess their stability and predictability for forecasting purposes.

Bottom-Up Approach (Decomposing Revenues Internally): This method breaks down revenues into granular components such as sales volume and price, or by product line, segment, geography, or other logical categories. It is often the preferred starting point because it aligns directly with the company's operations and business model. Further decomposition can reveal sub-drivers (e.g., number of stores × sales per store).

**Key Steps:**

1. Identify primary revenue streams from financial statements (e.g., net sales, membership fees).

2. Decompose each stream into drivers (e.g., average units × average price).

3. Calculate historical changes in drivers (absolute and percentage) to assess trends and stability.

4. Watch for signs of predictability (stable drivers) or volatility (external influences).

Case Study: Starbucks Corporation (Bottom-Up Example): Starbucks operates a global coffeehouse chain with revenues primarily from company-operated stores (beverages, food, and other items). Key drivers include the number of stores, comparable store sales growth (same-store sales, or SSS, which measures performance of stores open at least 13 months), and average ticket size (spend per transaction).

From 2013 to 2023, revenue from company-operated stores grew from $11.98B to $29.46B (146%), driven by a 92% increase in stores and cumulative SSS growth.

## 8.4 行业分类概述 Industry Classification Overview

Industry classification is the process of grouping companies that offer similar products or services. This helps analysts compare peers, forecast performance, and assess risks like competition or market trends. It's typically based on a "demand-side" view (what customers buy) rather than production methods.

### 8.4.1 美国上市公司所采用的分类标准 Classification Standards Used by US Stock Companies

For US-listed stocks (e.g., on NYSE or Nasdaq), the most common standard is the Global Industry Classification Standard (GICS), developed by MSCI and S\&P Dow Jones Indices. It's widely used for indexing (like S\&P 500 sectors), benchmarking, and research.

Hierarchy: GICS has 4 levels:

11 Sectors: Broad categories like Information Technology, Health Care, Financials, Consumer Discretionary, etc.

25 Industry Groups: Subdivisions, e.g., Software & Services under Information Technology.

74 Industries: More specific, e.g., Application Software.

## 9.1 证券监管机构 Securities Regulatory Organizations

The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) is the major watchdog of the securities industry. Congress created the SEC to regulate securities markets and to protect investors from  fraudulent and manipulative practices. All broker–dealers who transact business with investors and other broker–dealers must register with the SEC. And that registration means something: All broker–dealers have to comply with SEC rules or face censure (an official reprimand), limits on activity, their own suspension or suspension of one or more associated persons (such as a registered rep or principal), a fine, and/or having their registration revoked.

## 9.2 自律组织 Self-regulatory organizations

Due to the unscrupulous nature of some investors and registered representatives, the SEC’s job is overwhelming. Fortunately, a few self-regulatory organizations (SROs) are there to take some of the burden off of the SEC’s shoulders. Although membership isn’t mandatory, most broker–dealers are members of one or more SROs. SRO rules are usually stricter than those of the SEC. The four types of SROs we need to know are FINRA, MSRB, NYSE, and CBOE:

**»»Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA):** FINRA is the SRO responsible for the operation and regulation of the OTC market, investment banking ,NYSE trades, investment companies, limited partnerships, and so on. FINRA was created in 2007 and is a consolidation of the National Association of Securities Dealers (NASD) and the regulation and enforcement portions of the NYSE. FINRA is responsible for making sure that its members follow not only FINRA rules, but also the rules set forth by the SEC. Additionally, the FINRA is responsible for handling complaints against member firms and may take disciplinary action if necessary. FINRA is also responsible for administering securities exams such as the SIE.  FINRA has strict rules regarding filing of misleading, incomplete, or inaccurate information concerning membership, he firm’s registration, and the registration of member associates.

**»»Municipal Securities Rulemaking Board (MSRB):&#x20;**&#x54;he MSRB was established to develop rules that banks and securities firms have to follow when underwriting, selling, buying, and recommending municipal securities. The MSRB is subject to SEC oversight but does not enforce SEC rules. The MSRB makes rules for firms (and representatives) who sell municipal bonds but don’t enforce them; it leaves enforcement up to FINRA.

**»»New York Stock Exchange（NYSE）:&#x20;**&#x54;he NYSE is the oldest and largest stock exchange in the United States. The NYSE is responsible for listing securities, setting exchange policies, and supervising the exchange and member firms. The NYSE has the power to take disciplinary action against member firms.

**»»Chicago Board Options Exchange (CBOE):&#x20;**&#x54;he CBOE is an exchange that makes and enforces options exchange rules.

## 9.3 常见的违规行为以及监管处罚 Common Violations and Regulatory Sanctions

In the United States, regulations targeting individual securities practitioners (such as brokers, investment advisors, analysts, etc.) are extremely stringent. The primary regulatory bodies include the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA), and the Department of Justice (DOJ).

In the 2026 regulatory environment, the main violations for individual practitioners and their corresponding penalties are organized below:

### 9.3.1 **内幕交易与非法信息泄露 Insider Trading & Illegal Information Disclosure (Tipping)**

* **Regulation**: Practitioners are strictly prohibited from using "material non-public information" (MNPI) obtained through their positions for trading, or from disclosing such information to others (tipping).

* **Individual Penalties**:

  * **Civil Penalty**: The SEC can demand the disgorgement of all illegal profits and impose a fine of up to **3 times** the amount gained or loss avoided.

  * **Criminal Penalty**: The DOJ can file criminal charges, leading to a maximum of **20 years** in federal prison and individual criminal fines of up to **$5 million**.

  * **Industry Bar**: Permanent expulsion from the securities industry.

### 9.3.2 **私下交易与外接业务 Selling Away & Outside Business Activities**

* **Regulation**: Practitioners may not privately sell securities products unauthorized by their firm ("Selling Away") or engage in undisclosed Outside Business Activities (OBA) without written approval.

* **Individual Penalties**:

  * **FINRA Penalties**: Usually face suspensions ranging from several months to several years. If involved in a "Ponzi scheme" or serious fraud, they will be **permanently barred** from practice.

  * **Firm Penalties**: The employing firm will usually terminate the individual immediately and record the violation on the U5 form (termination notice), making future employment difficult.

### 9.3.3 **虚假陈述与误导性宣传 Misrepresentation & Omitting Facts**

* **Regulation**: Prohibited from concealing risks, exaggerating returns, or providing false financial data when selling securities to investors.

* **Individual Penalties**:

  * **Civil/Administrative**: Forfeiture of all commission income and substantial fines.

  * **Criminal**: If fraudulent intent (Scienter) is proven, the individual may be charged with wire fraud or securities fraud, leading to long-term imprisonment.

### 9.3.4 **盗取或挪用客户资金 Misappropriation or Embezzlement of Client Funds (Churning & Embezzlement)**

* **Regulation**: Strictly prohibited from moving client funds without authorization, or generating excessive commissions through high-frequency trading (Churning).

* **Individual Penalties**:

  * **Criminal Liability**: Almost certain to involve criminal prosecution, facing **5-20 years** of imprisonment.

  * **Restitution**: Must return all misappropriated funds and accrued interest.
